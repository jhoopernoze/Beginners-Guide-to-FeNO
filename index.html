Exhaled nitric oxide · a Noze field guide

Your breath carries a message from your airways.

When your airways are inflamed in a particular, allergy-like way, their lining releases more of a gas called nitric oxide (NO). Measuring it in one slow breath, a test called FeNO, is one of the few breath tests doctors already use. Your lungs have one big job

The airway tree

Your airways are an upside-down tree. One windpipe splits in two, then again and again, about 23 times, until the branches are thinner than a hair and end in air sacs. This chapter follows the air down that tree, and shows where nitric oxide joins it on the way back out.

1.1The airway tree

Twenty-three splits

Drag through the generations. The first 16 splits are plain pipes that carry air. From split 17, tiny air sacs start budding from the walls, and by 23 the tube is nothing but air sacs.

In plain terms

A "generation" is how many times the tube has split since the windpipe. Generation 0 is the windpipe itself; generation 23 is the last air sac. FeNO models call generations 0–16 "the tube" and 17–23 "the balloon", and most FeNO comes from the tube.

1.2The airway tree

Why the air slows to a crawl

Each split doubles the number of tubes. Their combined cross-section grows so fast that air slows from a brisk walk to almost nothing. Deep in the lung, gases stop flowing and start diffusing.

In plain terms

Picture a highway that splits into ever more lanes. The same traffic spread over more lanes moves slower. By the air sacs there are so many "lanes" that the air barely moves, and gas molecules simply wander across by diffusion.

1.3The airway tree

The three parts of a breath out

A breath out doesn't come from one place. First comes air that never left the windpipe, then a mix, then air from deep in the lung. Where a molecule is made decides which part of the breath carries it.

In plain terms

The first air out is "dead space" air from your mouth and windpipe. It never reached the air sacs, so it has no fresh carbon dioxide. The last air out is the best sample of the deep lung. NO, made in the airway walls, shows up early. CO₂, made in the body and dumped in the air sacs, shows up late.

Where our sources differWhere does exhaled carbon monoxide come from? Breathborne Ch. 2 groups CO with NO as compounds generated in the upper airways (phase II). Its Ch. 6 says exhaled CO "likely originates mainly from the alveolus", diffusing from carbon monoxide bound to hemoglobin in the blood. BB 2.2.1 BB 6.2

1.4The airway tree

Where NO joins the air

Every part of the airway adds some NO, but not equally. The nose makes the most, the lung's tubes a moderate amount, and the air sacs almost none. That's why the test is done through the mouth.

In plain terms

Think of the airway as a hallway lined with small NO leaks. The biggest leaks are in the nose. Blowing out through the mouth, against a little resistance that seals off the nose, means the sensor hears only the lungs.

1.5The airway tree

Warm, wet and blood-fed

By the time air reaches your air sacs it's at body temperature and fully humid. The airway walls do that work, fed by their own blood supply that comes straight from the aorta.

In plain terms

The airways are more than pipes. They warm and wet every breath, and they have their own small blood supply, separate from the huge flow of blood through the air sacs. Both affect what ends up in your breath.

2.2The airway lining

The NO factory

Inside each lining cell, enzymes called NO synthases turn an ordinary amino acid, L-arginine, into NO. Two versions idle along making a trickle. A third, iNOS, switches on with inflammation and floods the airway.

In plain terms

Your body builds NO with enzymes (protein machines) from arginine, an amino acid you get from food. Some machines are always on and make tiny amounts. One switches on with inflammation and makes a lot, and that's the one FeNO mostly reads. NOHA and citrulline are just the half-finished product and the leftover.

2.3The airway lining

The type 2 message chain

In allergy-type ("type 2") inflammation, one chain of immune messages switches on the NO factory while a parallel branch calls in eosinophils. Block a link and watch both readouts.

In plain terms

Allergy-type inflammation is a relay of chemical messages between cells. One branch switches on iNOS, which raises FeNO. Another calls in eosinophils, which show up in a blood test. Medicines that cut different links change different readings, which is why doctors use both.

2.4The airway lining

The asthma paradox

NO relaxes airways, and asthmatic airways are full of NO. So why do they clamp down so easily? Part of the answer: the protective NO and the inflammatory NO come from different enzymes, and inflammation tips the balance between them.

In plain terms

An asthmatic airway has two NO stories at once. The always-on enzymes make small, protective amounts that keep the muscle relaxed. Inflammation starves and suppresses them, while switching on iNOS, whose NO feeds the inflammation instead. More NO overall, less of the helpful kind.

Air sacs & blood

At the end of the airway tree, hundreds of millions of air sacs press air right up against blood. This is where oxygen gets in and carbon dioxide gets out, and where NO almost completely disappears. It's also the doorway for every blood-borne molecule that ends up in your breath.

3.1Air sacs & blood

The swap

Air goes in about one-fifth oxygen and comes out about one-seventh. The difference crossed into your blood, and carbon dioxide crossed the other way. Compare a breath in with a breath out.

In plain terms

Each air sac is a tiny balloon wrapped in blood vessels, with a wall far thinner than paper. Gases cross it by simply drifting from where there's more of them to where there's less.

Breath composition from Breathborne Ch. 1 BB 1.1. The book prints room-air oxygen as 20.1%; the usual figure is 20.9% outside library. Water vapour in room air varies with humidity.

3.2Air sacs & blood

Blood, the NO sink

NO made near the air sacs doesn't last: the blood streaming past soaks it up within seconds. Change the blood flow, the wall, or the NO production, and watch how much NO is left in the air sac.

In plain terms

Hemoglobin, the oxygen-carrier in red blood cells, grabs NO even more eagerly than oxygen. With a whole body's worth of blood flowing past every air sac, almost no NO survives there. That's why FeNO reports on the airways, not the air sacs.

3.3Air sacs & blood

Which molecules come from the blood?

Blood-borne molecules leave through the air sacs, but not all of them make it straight out. One number, how strongly a molecule prefers blood to air, decides whether it exchanges deep in the lung or trades back and forth with the airway walls.

In plain terms

Some molecules barely dissolve in blood and pop straight out into the air sacs, like isoprene. Others love water so much, like acetone, that they soak into the wet airway lining on the way out and come back out later. Breath-test designers need to know which kind they're chasing.

3.4Air sacs & blood

Alveolar NO as a clue

The tiny amount of NO left in the air sacs, CANO, can rise when the deep lung is inflamed or scarred, and it's been studied in conditions well beyond asthma, including some outside the lungs.

In plain terms

Measuring at several breath-out speeds (chapter 5.3) separates the airway NO from the air-sac NO. A raised air-sac value points to trouble deep in the lung rather than in the tubes.

3.5Air sacs & blood

Tiny droplets in every breath

Breath isn't only gas. Each breath carries hundreds to tens of thousands of microscopic droplets of lung-lining fluid. They form when tiny airways that closed during a deep breath out pop open again.

In plain terms

When you breathe all the way out, the smallest airways squeeze shut and their wet lining sticks together. When you breathe in, they snap open and the thin film of fluid bursts, like a soap film, into a mist of droplets that ride out on your next breath.

Two atoms and one spare electron. Nitric oxide is among the smallest molecules your body makes, it lasts only seconds, and it does opposite things depending on how much of it there is. 4.1The NO molecule

A molecule with a spare electron

Most molecules pair up all their electrons. NO has one left over, which makes it a "free radical": restless, reactive, and gone within seconds.

In plain terms

That lonely electron wants a partner, so NO reacts quickly with anything that can offer one: oxygen, metals like the iron in hemoglobin, and other radicals. Being tiny and uncharged, it also slips straight through cell membranes, so it needs no special receptor or door.

4.2The NO molecule

Five fates

A newly made NO molecule has a few seconds to do something. It can escape into the air, be captured by blood, burn up with an oxygen radical, park on a sulfur carrier, or flip a molecular switch. Only the first ever reaches a FeNO sensor.

In plain terms

Think of the NO made in your airway wall as a crowd leaving a stadium through five exits. FeNO counts only the people who leave by the "air" exit. The busier the other exits, the fewer reach the sensor.

Where does NO go?

4.3The NO molecule

Same molecule, opposite jobs

A little NO keeps airways open and clean. A lot of it, or none at all, tips the airway toward disease. Switch between the three and watch the lining.

In plain terms

At low levels NO is a helper: it relaxes airway muscle, speeds the cilia and helps fight germs. Block it, and airways tighten more easily. Flood the airway with it during inflammation, and it joins the damage: extra mucus, leaky vessels and harmful oxidants.

      4.4The NO molecule

      When NO turns toxic

      Inflamed airways make NO and oxygen radicals at the same time. When the two meet they form peroxynitrite, a harsh oxidant. Acid, meanwhile, can turn stored nitrite back into NO. Mix the ingredients and see what's left for your breath.

      In plain terms

      This is NO's dark side, called "nitrosative stress". Immune cells deliberately make these harsh chemicals to kill germs, but in chronic inflammation they also damage the airway itself. The lining fights back with antioxidants.

      Inside the analyzer

      How do you count a gas at a few parts per billion? The classic answer is to make it glow. Mix breath with ozone and every NO molecule releases a flash of light; count the flashes and you've counted the NO.

      In plain terms

      Ozone reacts with NO to make an "excited" molecule that sheds its extra energy as a tiny flash of light. A very sensitive light detector counts the flashes. More NO in the breath means more flashes per second.

      7.1Beyond NO

      Powers of ten: what's in one breath

      Four gases make up over 99% of a breath. Everything else is spread across a vast ladder of ever-smaller amounts: parts per million, parts per billion, parts per trillion. Slide the magnifier down the ladder.

      In plain terms

      Each rung on this ladder is ten times smaller than the one above. NO sits at the parts-per-billion rung: 17 NO molecules in every billion. Many other trace molecules sit even lower. Each one is a potential clue, if you can measure it.

      Where our sources differIsoprene's level: the e-nose book puts it in the parts-per-million band with acetone; Breathborne reports 22–234 ppb in healthy breath. The ladder uses Breathborne's measured range. BAMA T2.1 BB 23

      7.2Beyond NO

      Airway gas versus blood gas

      Where a molecule enters the breath decides how you have to sample it. NO comes from the airway wall and shows up early. CO₂ comes from the blood, through the air sacs, and shows up late. Acetone blurs the line.

      In plain terms

      Some breath molecules come from the airway walls; others come from the blood through the air sacs. Collect the wrong part of the breath and you'll miss what you're looking for, or dilute it.

      Noze lens · our synthesis, not a library claimFor airway-origin gases like NO, flow, mouthpiece resistance and nasal exclusion are part of the measurement. For alveolar gases, the priority shifts to discarding dead-space air and capturing end-exhaled gas. Water-soluble compounds like acetone blur the line because they trade with airway mucus on the way out, so breathing pattern and airway temperature matter too (BB 3.5). A multi-analyte device has to decide which of these regimes it is optimising for.

      In plain terms

      7.4Beyond NO

      How machines smell breath

      Some instruments separate and identify every compound; others, "electronic noses", recognise the overall pattern, the way your nose does. Try being the pattern-recogniser.

      In plain terms

      A lab instrument like GC-MS sorts a breath sample into its individual molecules and names them. An electronic nose is an array of cheaper sensors that each respond a bit differently; software learns which overall pattern goes with which condition, without naming the molecules.

      7.5Beyond NO

      Why breath tests are hard

      Hundreds of molecules, a few seconds of sampling, and no needles: breath looks like the perfect test. Yet very few breath tests are in routine use. Our library is candid about why.

      Noze lens · our synthesis, not a library claimFeNO shows what it takes for a breath marker to become a routine test: a known biological source (one enzyme in one tissue), a physical model of how it reaches the mouth (chapter 5.2), a standardised manoeuvre (50 mL/s), a clear clinical question (is this type 2 inflammation?), and devices that got smaller and cheaper. Each of the hurdles on this page is one of those ingredients missing for other molecules. That's a useful checklist for any new breath analyte.
